Chapter 1 — Foundations
Purpose

The MFEE Learning Center Library exists to help every learner discover the right book at the right moment.

Discovery depends on language.

If every teacher, librarian, volunteer, tutor, family member, and AI system describes books differently, discovery becomes inconsistent.

The purpose of the Controlled Vocabularies is to establish a shared language for describing books throughout the Living Library.

These vocabularies support:

Consistent cataloging
Reliable AI classification
Better search
Better recommendations
Better reporting
Better instructional planning

At the same time, the Living Library recognizes that no controlled vocabulary can fully describe a book.

For that reason, every controlled vocabulary is complemented by Community Voices, where readers contribute their own experiences and perspectives.

Controlled vocabularies create consistency.

Community Voices preserve richness.

Together they help every Book Profile tell its complete story.

Why Controlled Vocabularies Matter

Imagine three people describing the same book.

A teacher says:

"This would be wonderful for teaching perseverance."

A parent says:

"It's about never giving up."

A student says:

"It's about believing in yourself."

All three are describing the same underlying idea.

Without a shared vocabulary, these books become difficult to discover consistently.

Controlled vocabularies allow different people to speak a common language while still preserving their unique voices through Community Knowledge.

The Living Library values both.

Design Principles

Every controlled vocabulary should be:

Consistent

The same concept should always be described using the same term.

Human-Centered

The vocabulary should make sense to educators, librarians, families, students, and volunteers—not only to computers.

Educationally Meaningful

Every term should help someone teach, learn, recommend, or discover a book.

If a term doesn't improve discovery or learning, it probably doesn't belong.

AI-Friendly

Artificial intelligence should classify books using these standardized vocabularies whenever possible.

Consistency improves the quality of recommendations.

Community-Friendly

Controlled vocabularies describe books.

Community Voices describe experiences.

The two complement one another.

They should never compete.

Flexible

Language evolves.

The Controlled Vocabularies should be reviewed and refined as the library grows, educational practices change, and the community identifies new needs.

The Living Library Taxonomy

The Living Library currently defines six primary controlled vocabularies.

Vocabulary	Guiding Question
Library Classification	Where does this book belong?
Subjects	What is this book about?
Themes	What ideas does this book explore?
Representation	Whose experiences are represented?
Instructional Supports	How can this book support learning?
Discovery	How might someone discover this book?

Each vocabulary serves a different purpose.

Together they create a richer understanding of every book.

Relationships Between the Vocabularies

Each vocabulary answers a different question.

Together they create a complete picture of a book.

For example:

A book may have:

Subject: Animals
Theme: Friendship
Representation: Rural Communities
Instructional Support: Read Aloud
Discovery: Funny Books
Library Classification: Picture Books

No single vocabulary tells the entire story.

Together they help readers discover books in meaningful ways.

Chapter 2 — Subjects
Guiding Question

What is this book about?

Purpose

The Subject Vocabulary describes the primary topics, disciplines, people, places, events, and concepts presented within a book.

Subjects answer a simple but essential question:

What is this book about?

Unlike Themes, which describe the ideas a book explores, Subjects describe the observable content of the book.

For example:

A book about a rabbit making friends might have:

Subject: Rabbits

Theme: Friendship

Both are true.

They answer different questions.

Why Subjects Matter

Subjects are often the first way readers search for books.

A child may ask:

"Do you have books about dinosaurs?"

A teacher may ask:

"I need books about weather."

A tutor may ask:

"Do you have books about fractions?"

Subjects make those searches possible.

They support:

Browsing
Curriculum planning
Research
Classroom instruction
Independent reading
AI recommendations
Design Principles
Describe What Is Present

Subjects should describe the actual content of the book.

They should not describe the lesson or message.

Example:

A story about two foxes learning to cooperate.

Subject:

Foxes

Not:

Friendship

Friendship is a Theme.

Be Specific

Whenever possible, choose the most specific subject available.

Instead of:

Animals

Prefer:

Bears

Ocean Animals

Farm Animals

Birds

Specific subjects improve discovery.

Use Multiple Subjects

Most books contain more than one subject.

For example:

A nonfiction book about butterflies may include:

Butterflies
Insects
Metamorphosis
Nature

Together these create a richer description.

Support Curriculum

Subjects should align with how educators organize instruction whenever possible.

Examples include:

Science

Mathematics

Social Studies

History

Geography

Health

Arts

Language

This makes classroom planning easier.

Relationship to Other Vocabularies

Subjects answer:

What is this book about?

Themes answer:

What ideas does this book explore?

Representation answers:

Whose experiences are represented?

Instructional Supports answer:

How can this book be used?

Discovery answers:

How might someone find it?

Each vocabulary answers a different question.

Together they create a richer understanding of every book.

Living Library Principle

Subjects should make books easier to find.

They should never limit how readers experience a story.

Every reader may discover something different in the same book.

Subjects describe what is present.

Readers discover what is meaningful.

Simplicity Creates Discovery

The purpose of the Living Library is not to describe every possible characteristic of a book.

Its purpose is to help people discover the right book at the right moment.

Every controlled vocabulary should be intentionally limited.

Every approved term should earn its place by making discovery easier, not more complicated.

When faced with two equally useful approaches, the Living Library prefers the simpler one.

A smaller, thoughtfully curated vocabulary creates more consistent cataloging, better AI recommendations, and a better experience for readers.

Less, chosen well, is more powerful than more, chosen indiscriminately.

Every word we choose shapes how readers discover books.
